Canary Catch a quantum cloud that cheats · Qollab × IonQ hackathon

A quantum computer's answer can't be checked. That's the point of it.
So how do you know your quantum cloud didn't cheat?

You rent quantum computers over the cloud. A provider could quietly run a cheaper machine, skip part of your job, or send back made-up numbers, and no normal computer can redo the work to catch it. Canary hides traps inside your job: circuits that look like the rest but whose answer you already know. Then it turns the traps into a receipt that says how many of your answers you can trust.

What Canary is
Spot-checking with hidden traps, plus a statistical receipt. If the cloud can't tell traps from real jobs, the traps are a random sample of your batch, so the number of dead traps bounds the number of wrong answers (a 95% hypergeometric bound on this batch). That's the accreditation idea from Ferracin, Kapourniotis and Datta (2019). Every circuit is Pauli-twirled before sending, so machine errors hit traps and real jobs alike.

    
What it isn't
Not full verification. A server that reads the gate angles can spot Clifford traps. Hiding them needs blind quantum computing (Fitzsimons & Kashefi, 2017), and checking a quantum computer with only a classical one needs Mahadev's protocol (2018). For a real trap compiler, see VeriQloud's Trapify. Cloud A is the backend you picked; the cheating clouds are simulated on top of it.
